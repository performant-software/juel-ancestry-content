                  - text: "UVA’s Gibbons Project, the successor to two presidential commissions researching slavery and the age of segregation at the university, is dedicated to developing materials that make accessible and intelligible the vast array of archival materials identifying those enslaved at UVA and in the surrounding region.\_\n\nEnslavement and Ancestry at UVA and Beyond represents our effort to answer calls from descendants to make documents accessible, to make them legible, to make them searchable, and to create something that allows descendants and researchers to work forward and backward in time as they identify ancestors and build family trees.\_\n\nAs this project grew out of work started by the President’s Commission on Slavery and the University (hotlink to slavery.virginia.edu) and was initially focused on identifying as many of the estimated four thousand or more enslaved people who lived and labored for some time at UVA between 1817 and 1865, the project’s core initial focus has been on continuing that work.\_\n\nWe have partnered with the Jefferson’s University-The Early Life project, which has created a thickly descriptive and resource-rich digital humanities site about the university’s first half century, to highlight the stories of the enslaved unsung founders of the university.\_\n\nIn continuing collaboration with UVA’s descendant community as represented by the non-profit Descendants of Enslaved Communities of Virginia (DEC-VA) \\[[https://www.decva.org/](https://www.decva.org/)] the materials here began with slavery at UVA but continue to expand to Central Virginia and beyond and are not limited to materials that are directly related to UVA. We continue to add new materials as our research team uncovers new materials.\n"

                      This web project represents a collaboration between the
                      Jefferson’s University-The Early Life (JUEL) team and the
                      University of Virginia’s Gibbons Project. Bringing
                      together a trove of personal and administrative documents,
                      as well as archival images of the university and
                      three-dimensional digital renderings, JUEL invites users
                      to discover the people and places of the University’s
                      early years, stretching from its founding in 1819 through
                      the end of the Civil War. That project, an expansive
                      archive of documents, 3D models, and interpretive
                      materials, yields rich historical data and also brings to
                      life the Academical Village’s built landscape as
                      nineteenth century people would have experienced it. The
                      Gibbons Project works to expand what we know about slavery
                      at the University of Virginia, to continue identifying and
                      connecting with living descendants, and to developing
                      digital humanities ensuring documentary accessibility.
