              ## The Enslaved


              Enslaved individuals were responsible for a significant portion of
              the construction and maintenance of the University until 1865.
              Although annual tax assessments and decennial censuses enumerated
              the enslaved at UVA, they do not provide any other identifiers.
              University financial records frequently document the names of the
              enslavers who rented people to the school, often provide some
              description of the labor performed, and sometimes include names or
              partial names of enslaved people. There are multiple ways to begin
              research: searching by names of enslavers or those enslaved, by
              names of free Blacks and freedpeople, or by browsing the documents
              themselves. We continue to add new materials as the research
              continues.

          - text: "## Free Blacks and Freedpeople\n\n##### *Free Black People*\n\nThis project offers opportunities to research and learn more about both free Black people in Central Virginia and about formerly enslaved people in the years after passage of the 13th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution abolishing slavery. Free Black people in Virginia after 1790 typically comprised less than 3% of the total population (primarily free whites and enslaved people). Their lives, families, and communities were often intertwined with those of the enslaved. Although few in number, their experiences and successes in creating intergenerational security while navigating the many perils of living as free and Black in a slave state meant that their knowledge and resources often provided foundational support for post-emancipation Black Virginians.\_\n"

              ##### *Freedpeople*


              With the 1865 surrender of Confederate armies and passage of the
              13th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution abolishing slavery, fully
              one third of Virginia’s population, nearly a half a million
              people, claimed freedom and began the hard work of reconstituting
              families, seeking education, finding wage labor, purchasing land
              and homes, and building formal community organizations including
              churches and mutual aid societies. For the first time, they
              appeared with full names in county and municipal records, in
              school matriculant lists, and in church records. Many returned to
              work for wages at UVA. The archival resources in this project seek
              to reveal their stories both in freedom and earlier as enslaved
              people.

          - text: "## Browse and Explore the Primary Sources\n\nThis project seeks to make accessible thousands of documents that identify enslaved people and provide details about their lives and experiences. Those include official university records, bills of sale, advertisements, business ledgers, letters, diaries, and journals. They also include county court marriage data, probate records, property deeds, civil suit and criminal court papers, and also state government documents. All the primary source records found here are available as full-text transcripts. In many cases, images of the original primary documents are also available.\_The goals are to allow anyone anywhere to see the original documents, read the transcriptions, and connected to related source materials through our robust search function. We continue to find, transcribe, and digitize thousands of documents.\n"
